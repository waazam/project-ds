# Research: Stairs in the Woods

Background research for Project DS. This file covers the reference sources, the wider phenomenon and its real-world explanations, what we must not copy, and what we take for design.
Researched 2026-09-18.

## Analysis (general concepts only)

### Atmosphere
- The dread comes from the ordinary. The woods look normal, and so does the anomaly. Nothing glows.
- The feeling is procedural and tired, more like a work diary than a campfire story. The strangeness sits between routine tasks.
- Remoteness does much of the work. The anomalies happen far past where roads and phone signal end, where nobody can check the story.

### How the stairs are described
- They are domestic, as if lifted whole out of a house. The scare is that they belong to a *home*, not to ruins.
- They are complete and in good condition. A ruined stair is history, but a clean one is a question.
- They have a human scale, about one storey tall, and they lead nowhere.
- They are banal and specific. One or two plain material details (finish, colour, step count) do more than any description of their strangeness.
- The real sculpture thread shows the same image in daylight. A staircase to the treetops by a pool reads as eerie until someone names it. Even after that, the thread still likes the idea of it.

### Forest isolation
- Distance is measured in days of walking, not in miles.
- There is no help, no witnesses, and no signal. The environment itself is the only authority.
- Search and rescue framing gives a reason to be off-trail. We need our own reason, and our protagonist premise provides one.

### Psychological reactions
- Everyone treats the anomaly as normal. Characters have filed it away, and that frightens more than panic would.
- Nobody explains the rules they pass on. They obey them without understanding them.
- The narrator knows how it sounds, second-guesses the memory, and plays it down.
- The characters stay curious and don't act on it. The reader wants to climb, and the characters won't.

### Silence
- Silence is not the source's main device. Its unease comes more from missing sounds (a scent trail that stops, sounds that fade) and wrong sounds (an animal noise from a throat that isn't an animal's).
- Across the wider folklore, "the woods went quiet" is a common motif. It has a real basis: songbirds and insects do go quiet or give alarm calls when a predator is near. People read silence as a warning even when they can't name why.
- **Our opportunity:** nobody has made silence a *system*. Silence that is directional, measurable and progressive is ours to own.

### Environmental anomalies (generic categories)
- Objects in impossible places, such as belongings too high up or too far away.
- People found where they could not have walked.
- Tracks and scent that stop.
- Sounds that imitate something, badly.
- Structures that belong somewhere else.

### Storytelling techniques
- Short, separate anecdotes, each one unresolved. They build a pattern without any explanation.
- A plain, unliterary first-person voice. Casual swearing sits next to clinical detail.
- The authority is implied: supervisors forbid, trainers pass things on, and nobody knows why.
- The source does not over-explain, and critics agree it weakens when it escalates into gore and spectacle. **Restraint scares. Escalation makes it absurd.**
- It encourages audience participation. Readers want to add their own sightings, as the photo thread shows.
- The real-world rebuttal is weak on purpose. "It's just an art piece" or "it's just an old homesite" never fully clears the chill.

## Real-world explanations (plausibility fuel)
- **Displaced homesteads.** Parks were often formed by condemning private land. Shenandoah alone took over 1,000 tracts and displaced 500+ families in the 1920s and 30s. Homes were torn down or burned. What remains is foundations, a lone stone chimney, orchard trees, family graveyards and **front steps leading up to nothing.**
- **CCC work, 1933 to 1942.** Civilian Conservation Corps crews built stone stairs, retaining walls, lookouts, shelters and trail steps across parks. Many of these were later abandoned and overgrown, so a well-made stone stair can turn up where no trail runs any more.
- **Fire lookouts.** Towers were decommissioned and their cabs removed, leaving footings, concrete pads and the lowest flights of steps.
- **Fires.** After a burn, stone and masonry survive. Chimneys and stairs are often the last parts standing.
- **Relocated and staged objects.** Art installations, film sets and salvaged stairs get dumped or left behind.
- **Design point:** every real explanation involves *people who used to be there*. The horror we want is the explanation that almost works.

## DO NOT USE

These belong to the sources or their adaptations. Nobody on the team should put them in text, art, audio or code names.

**From the nosleep series:**
- A search and rescue officer, a Forest Service employee, or any rescuer or official as the narrator or framing device.
- "Don't go near them, don't touch them, don't climb them, just ignore them", or any ranger or supervisor rule telling you to ignore the stairs, or any wording like it.
- Superiors ordering silence, "we don't talk about it", or the stairs as a normal part of the job.
- Stairs appearing on a fixed share of calls, "every fifth call", or at a set depth such as 30–40 miles.
- **Carpeted stairs**, especially beige carpet, and "about ten steps".
- The "like the stairs in your house, cut out and dropped in the forest" comparison.
- A faceless man or faceless figures, especially on peaks or ridges.
- Bear-like humanoids.
- An entity repeating an animal noise (such as "meow") in a flat, buzzing or electronic voice.
- A walking stick or cane high up in a tree.
- A child found impossibly far away, a toddler in a berry patch, a hiker dying in a tree well.
- Bodies with holes punched through organs, people impaled on trees, time-travel anecdotes.
- A numbered "part 1, 2, 3" set of short incident posts as our story structure.

**From the adaptation and related works:**
- *Channel Zero: Butcher's Block*: stairs as a door to an underground realm, a family living below, cannibalism.
- "Missing 411"-style lore: clusters of disappearances, missing shoes, the "it's been hushed up" conspiracy.

**From the photo thread:**
- Do not copy *Observatory* by Bruce Allan: a freestanding timber stair beside a pool that rises to canopy height. It is a real, copyrighted artwork. None of our stairs should reproduce its structure.

**General tropes:** skinwalkers, wendigos, "the rake", a figure at the edge of the flashlight beam, fake news-report found footage, and "the stairs are a portal" as the explanation.

## Design takeaways

### Silence
- **Silence is the monster's footprint.** The player hears the stair before seeing it, by hearing *less*. This is the one idea we own. Build every system around it.
- **Remove sounds in layers, not all at once.** Nature sounds should drop category by category (the buses: `Birds`, `Insects`, `Wind`, `Distant`, `Water`). Players learn the order and start to read distance by ear.
- **Silence has a texture.** Once nature is gone, bring up quiet sounds that were always there: the player's breath, cloth rustle, blood-in-ears hum, a faint room tone on `Unnatural`. True digital silence reads as a bug. Near-silence reads as a place.
- **Getting sound back matters as much as losing it.** Birds starting up again one at a time should feel like relief the first time and like a trap the fourth time.
- **Never add a sting when a stair appears.** No music hit. The quiet is the scare.

### Pacing
- Follow the source's strength and not its weakness: get stranger over time, never gorier.
- There should be long stretches of ordinary park between anomalies. A PS2-era horror game can afford 10 minutes of hiking. Earned boredom makes the quiet readable.
- Put one new rule in each act. Never explain the old rules; only show that they have changed.
- Cap the spectacle. There should be no creature reveal the player could film and post as "the monster".

### "Did I see that?"
- The first stairs appear only in peripheral or half-hidden views: through trunks, at dusk, in fog, in the background of the player's own photo.
- Anomalies should be deniable on first sight. Is that a CCC step, or a stair? Make the real remnants common enough that the fake ones hide among them.
- Reverse something small when the player looks back: a stair missing on the return trip, one extra step, a trail marker facing the other way.

### Ordinariness
- Lean on the lore of real parks: condemned homesites, CCC stonework, lookouts, rental cabins, trailhead register boxes, laminated notices, bear-box rules. The world should feel like a real, slightly underfunded park.
- Each stair gets one plain material detail (a paint colour, a nail, a worn tread), and never gets a glow or a sigil.
- Documents should read like work: maintenance tickets, register entries, invoices, lost-and-found tags. The strangeness goes in the margins.

### Sound (production notes)
- The Unnatural bus should be *interior* sound in an exterior space, such as room tone, a fridge hum or a clock. This is cheap, original and unsettling.
- Keep the player's own sounds loud and dry inside silence zones. Footsteps on leaf litter become the loudest thing in the world, and the player starts sneaking without being told to.
- Put low-bitrate, low-sample-rate mono loops on purpose into the "lost PS2 disc" feel. A slightly crunchy silence is a style choice.
